Opinion · The New York Times · May 3, 2026
Why the A.I. Job Apocalypse (Probably) Won't Happen
By Ezra Klein  ·  Opinion Columnist

A Quinnipiac poll in March found that 70 percent of Americans think that artificial intelligence will lead to fewer job opportunities for human beings, up from 56 percent a year ago. Thirty percent say they're worried for their own jobs. And why not? Warnings of a coming labor market apocalypse feature prominently in the remarks of A.I. leaders.

Dario Amodei, the chief executive of Anthropic, says that up to half of all entry-level white collar jobs will dissolve within the next five years. Mustafa Suleyman, the chief executive of Microsoft AI, believes that most white-collar work will "be fully automated by an A.I. within the next 12 to 18 months." OpenAI released a policy paper calling for a 32-hour workweek so that A.I. creates mass leisure rather than mass joblessness. Outside my window at The New York Times, there is a large billboard for an A.I. company I've never heard of that reads: "Stop Hiring Humans."

Thanks.

If you believe the story the A.I. labs are telling, it's hard to see what stands between us and mass unemployment. A.I. has been designed to cheaply mimic what human beings can do on a computer, but never needs to sleep, never tries to form a union and often outperforms real people on real tasks; of course companies will want to replace human beings with this human-being-replacement machine. Maybe they already are. Tech companies like Block, Meta, Oracle and Microsoft are laying off or buying out workers and naming A.I. as the reason.

But it's worth being cautious. These tech companies might be unwinding a hiring binge and telling the stock market the tale likeliest to excite or appease investors. The A.I. leaders might understand neural nets better than they understand labor markets — or they might have bought too deeply into their own marketing materials.

For one thing, the macrodata isn't matching the anecdata: The unemployment rate was 4.3 percent in March 2026; in March of 2020, it was 4.4 percent. Average hourly earnings are stable. Claude Code is a marvel, yet demand for software engineers is booming. Maybe mass layoffs are coming. But maybe not.

Economists, I've found, are quite skeptical that mass joblessness is on the horizon. In "What Will Be Scarce?," Alex Imas, an economist at the University of Chicago, tries to clarify the mistake most A.I. discourse, in his view, makes. "The answer to any question about the future economics of advanced A.I. begins with identifying what becomes scarce," Imas writes.

For most of human history, calories were scarce. Our energy went into finding or growing food. Agriculture steadily made food more plentiful and goods became scarce. Then goods were scarce; hand-me-down clothes were common and tools were expensive. Innovations in technology and manufacturing made goods cheaper. Then, technical knowledge became scarce: Doctors, lawyers and software engineers are paid high salaries because of the rarity of what they know. The fear is that A.I. will make knowledge plentiful; that it will turn the fruits of learning into a commodity as surely as manufacturing turned clothing into a commodity and industrial agriculture made strawberries commonplace.

But something is always scarce. People are looking at the economy as it exists and asking which tasks A.I. can do; they should be asking which jobs people won't want A.I. doing, or which services A.I. will make us want more of.

Here is a poetic finding from econometrics: As the rich get richer, they want more from other humans, not less. They "shift their spending toward goods and services where the human element, the experience or the social meaning matters more," Imas writes. They seek out clothing with a story, food with a provenance, doctors who make house calls, therapists who make them feel seen, tutors who know their children and personal trainers who work around their injuries. This, Imas says, is "the relational sector" of the economy, and it will explode. Instead of so many human beings working with computers, they will work with other human beings.

The more automation there is, the more people value a human's touch. Take coffee. It was once laborious to make espresso at home. Now, Nespresso machines are everywhere. Has that led to Starbucks closing and neighborhood coffee spots dropping their prices? Of course not. There are more baristas than ever. There are more coffee shops than ever. Coffee as a commodity led to more demand for coffee as an experience. "The fact that the good is scarce is exactly what gives it meaning," Imas writes.

Imas's story suggests a place where human labor might move amid mass automation: toward more human roles. But it's also possible that human labor won't need to move that much at all.

In 1979, VisiCalc, the first electronic spreadsheet, was released for the Apple II. It could do in minutes what previously took teams of accountants days. There were predictions of mass unemployment for bookkeepers. Instead, the number of accountants quadrupled over the next 40 years. "The spreadsheet didn't replace the accountant," Eldar Maksymov writes. "It unleashed latent demand for financial intelligence that had been there all along, waiting for costs to fall far enough to be satisfied."

Maksymov, an accounting professor at Arizona State University, is describing the "Jevons Paradox," so named for William Stanley Jevons, a British economist. Jevons, writing in 1865, was interested in Britain's use of coal. Not long before, James Watt had invented a new-and-improved steam engine, which generated more than twice as much power from a given amount of coal. But instead of cutting Britain's use of coal, demand for coal tripled. Cheap coal didn't lead to less coal being used; it led to coal being used for more things than anyone had previously thought possible.

This, Maksymov thinks, is what A.I. is likely to do even in the industries most exposed to its disruption. He thinks that, in part, because it happened before. "In every major occupational group that adopted computers heavily, employment grew faster than in groups that did not," he writes. "Computers eliminated specific tasks within jobs — but the resulting cost reductions created so much new demand that the occupations expanded overall." Computers can do much that humans once did, but they didn't put humans out of work. The ability to do more made people realize there was more to do.

This is fairly common. When I started my podcast, 10 years ago, I was its only researcher; now I have an extraordinary team of people who help me prepare episodes. Has that made my job easier? Not in the least. I spend far more time researching and prepping because they bring me so much more to absorb and consider, and I choose to do more challenging episodes because I am confident I can do them.

All the enthusiastic A.I. adopters I know are working harder than ever because there is more they can do. Whether they are working smarter is arguable. Studies differ on whether A.I. is making people more productive or simply giving them (and their bosses) the illusion of productivity. Slowly reading a difficult book is far better than rapidly absorbing summaries of 12 books; struggling through a first draft will lead you to more new ideas than editing five A.I.-written drafts. It's my belief that the feeling of efficiency should be mistrusted. The people I know who have small armies of A.I. agents working on their behalf certainly feel more productive, but I have not noticed their work improving. In some cases, it has clearly declined.

But I digress. Ethan Mollick, a professor at the Wharton School, once told me about a test he used for A.I.: Is it better than your best available human? The question, as he saw it, wasn't whether A.I. was better than the best editor or coder or therapist or researcher or dermatologist or travel agent; it was whether it was better than the best such person you could call in your moment of need.

Over the past year, I have watched the A.I.s I use become better than my best available person quite often. I have an amazing editor, but he needs to sleep and work with other writers; I have a wonderful therapist, but I see her once a week, if that; I have access to good doctors, but it takes work to see them. Perhaps I had hit the event horizon I had been warned of, and A.I. would begin replacing the humans in my life.

But the opposite happened. The better the A.I. got, the more I had to discuss with the humans in my life. The A.I. thought my symptoms were concerning, so I made an appointment with my doctor (allergies, it turned out); it had a good insight on a personal challenge, and that opened a new conversation with my therapist; it allowed me to validate a research idea, and that opened up a new question to explore with my editor; A.I. has made it possible to caption videos easily, and now I work with more video editors. The better my A.I. has gotten, the more I've wanted from the human beings around me — and from myself.

While I don't believe full automation of the economy, or even mass unemployment, is likely, I don't totally discount the possibility. A.I. is a different kind of technology than what has come before: Perhaps its flexibility and conversational nature will make it a substitute when previous tools have proven to be complements. What's likelier, though, is that A.I. doesn't take all, or most, of the jobs. But it does take some. And that, strangely, is the possibility we're least prepared for.

A world where A.I. displaces eight million workers might be harder to handle than a world where it displaces 80 million workers. A mass unemployment event would force a wholesale restructuring of our economy. Covid offers an example: The shock was so total that we didn't revert to our normal habit of blaming workers for their misfortunes; instead, we created an unprecedented architecture of income support that made lockdown a period of unexpected prosperity for millions of workers.

We are crueler when the displacement is more limited. The best estimates of job loss from competition with China put it around two million jobs. That's small in the context of the entire U.S. economy, where roughly five million people are hired each month and about five million people leave or lose their jobs each month. But it was devastating for particular communities, and we did very, very little to help them.

If everyone with marketing degrees or trucking jobs found themselves jobless, we might act; if unemployment among marketers or truckers merely triples, we will do what we've done in the past: Suggest it's their fault, give them a few months of unemployment insurance or some retraining options that don't work and then mostly ignore their plight.

Then there's the reality that even as A.I. makes relational skills more valuable, it may make those skills rarer. Young people have gone from spending about 12 hours a week with friends in 2003 to about five hours a week in 2024. The number of high school seniors who report going on a date fell from 80 percent in 2000 to 46 percent in 2024. About a quarter of Gen Z-ers report that they haven't had sex in the last year. A.I. could be a handmaiden of this social dissolution, offering a digital simulacrum of friendships and relationships without opening people to the beauty and agony of real relationships, in which we learn to relate to other people who are truly other people, and whose desires are not simply extensions of our own.

If Imas is right — and I think he is — our ability to relate, sensitively and deeply, to other human beings will be a central and valuable skill. That, I fear, is the exact skill we are breaking down in the young.

When I am feeling optimistic about the world A.I. might make possible, I imagine a world in which we are richer than we are today and are encouraged to live more fundamentally human lives, doing more fundamentally human things. When I am feeling pessimistic, I imagine something like that same world, but the wealth will be hoarded and we will value a depth of human connection that we no longer know how to provide.

Labor Economics · Human Capital Theory

The economic premium for doctors, lawyers, and software engineers reflects both the scarcity of specialized knowledge and the signaling value of credentialing. Gary Becker's human capital theory (1964) formalized why investing in knowledge earns returns analogous to physical capital investment.

Alex Imas, "What Will Be Scarce?" · 2025–2026

"The fact that the good is scarce is exactly what gives it meaning. Abundance destroys the very quality that makes something valuable in a world that prizes authenticity, effort, and human presence."

Economic Concept
The Jevons Paradox — Efficiency Increases Consumption
William Stanley Jevons, "The Coal Question" · 1865

"It is a confusion of ideas to suppose that the economical use of fuel is equivalent to diminished consumption. The very contrary is the truth." — W.S. Jevons, The Coal Question, 1865

The paradox: James Watt's improved steam engine (c. 1769–1782) used coal more efficiently than earlier designs. But instead of reducing Britain's total coal consumption, demand for coal tripled — because cheaper power enabled new industries and new applications no one had previously considered economical.

Modern applications: fuel-efficient cars → more driving (rebound effect); efficient lighting → more hours of light use; cheaper computation → vastly more computation. The Jevons Paradox is now central to energy economics debates about whether efficiency gains can reduce environmental impact without price mechanisms.

The AI application: if AI makes cognitive work cheaper, demand for cognitive work outputs may expand faster than the efficiency gains — meaning more total AI use, not replacement of human activity.

Person
William Stanley Jevons (1835–1882) — Economist & Logician
British, 1835–1882 · Manchester & University College London

Jevons (1835–1882) was an English economist and logician who transformed economic theory. His Theory of Political Economy (1871) established marginal utility theory. His Coal Question (1865) introduced what became the Jevons Paradox. He invented the "Logic Piano," an early mechanical computing device.

Jevons was born in Liverpool to a family of merchants. He worked as a metallurgical assayer in Sydney before returning to academia, holding professorships at Owens College Manchester and University College London. Irving Fisher called his Theory of Political Economy "the start of the mathematical method in economics."

He died by drowning in 1882 at age 46. Alfred Marshall praised his work as having "more constructive force than any, save that of Ricardo." His concerns in The Coal Question about Britain's coal dependence were partly validated — Britain's coal industry peaked in the early 20th century.

"In every major occupational group that adopted computers heavily, employment grew faster"
Eldar Maksymov, citing broader labor economics literature · 2025–2026

"In every major occupational group that adopted computers heavily, employment grew faster than in groups that did not. Computers eliminated specific tasks within jobs — but the resulting cost reductions created so much new demand that the occupations expanded overall."

This empirical claim draws on a substantial labor economics literature. David Autor's foundational work on task-biased technological change (with Lawrence Katz, Alan Krueger, and others) documents how computerization polarized the labor market: it automated middle-skill routine tasks (bookkeeping, clerical work) while expanding both high-skill (professional) and low-skill (service) employment.

The Autor framework is more nuanced than Maksymov's summary: computers did eliminate many middle-skill jobs (creating the "hollowing out" of the middle class) while expanding occupations at the top and bottom. The net employment effect was positive, but the distributional effect was worsening inequality — something Klein doesn't address here.

Why this deserves a hyperlink This is an empirical claim about computers and employment that has a large academic literature behind it. The nuances — especially Autor's polarization thesis — significantly complicate the optimistic "computers created more jobs" narrative.
Research Summary
Mixed Evidence on AI Productivity — Studies Conflict
Multiple research institutions · 2023–2026

MIT (2023): AI assistance improved customer service worker productivity 14% on average, with larger gains for lower-skill workers. Stanford/NBER (2023, GitHub Copilot): 55% faster task completion. But Microsoft Research (2024): "AI makes people think less" — reliance on AI reduces critical thinking. Goldman Sachs (2023): "Most jobs will be partly affected but few will be fully automated."

The research genuinely is mixed. Early studies (2023) showed strong productivity gains in narrow, measurable tasks. Later studies began documenting "automation complacency" — over-reliance on AI that degrades human skill over time. The medium-term picture is unclear: does AI raise the productivity floor (making mediocre workers better) or lower the productivity ceiling (making excellent workers worse by reducing the hard work that produces excellence)?

Klein's observation that "slowly reading a difficult book is far better than rapidly absorbing summaries" is consistent with cognitive science research on depth of processing — but this claim itself lacks a citation.

Why this deserves a hyperlink "Studies differ" is doing a lot of work here. Readers deserve to know which studies, what they found, and what the methodological limitations are. This is one of the most actively contested empirical questions in economics right now.

Framework / Concept
"Is it better than your best available human?"
Ethan Mollick, Wharton School · Shared privately with Klein

"The question wasn't whether AI was better than the best editor or coder or therapist or researcher or dermatologist or travel agent; it was whether it was better than the best such person you could call in your moment of need."

This is a paraphrase of a private conversation, but it reflects a framework Mollick has developed publicly. In Co-Intelligence and his newsletter, he argues that AI's relevant comparison is not "the best expert in the world" but "the expert you can actually access" — democratizing access to expertise that was previously available only to the wealthy or well-connected.

This framing reframes the AI debate from replacement to accessibility — a genuinely important conceptual shift. It also raises equity questions: if AI provides everyone with a "brilliant friend," does that reduce demand for actual human professionals, or does it, as Mollick and Klein argue, increase awareness of when you need them?

Historical Event
COVID-19 Income Support — CARES Act & Expanded Unemployment
U.S. Congress · March 2020 (CARES Act) · ~$5 trillion total stimulus

The CARES Act (March 2020) created $600/week supplemental unemployment benefits, Paycheck Protection Program loans, and direct stimulus payments. The American Rescue Plan (March 2021) extended and expanded these programs. Together they represented the largest peacetime economic intervention in U.S. history.

Klein's claim that lockdown was "a period of unexpected prosperity for millions of workers" is supported by data: real median household income fell only slightly in 2020 despite the worst unemployment crisis since the Great Depression. The poverty rate actually declined in 2020 and 2021 due to transfers.

However, the programs were not without costs: record national debt expansion, inflationary pressures in 2022–2023, and significant fraud in PPP distribution (estimated $100–$200 billion in fraudulent loans). Klein's point about "unprecedented architecture of income support" is accurate but elides these costs.

Why this deserves a hyperlink Klein uses COVID policy as his model for how society should respond to mass unemployment. Readers deserve to see the full policy record — what was done, what it cost, and what its distributional effects were — to evaluate whether it's replicable.
Economic Research
The "China Shock" — ~2 Million Jobs Lost to Import Competition
David Autor, David Dorn, Gordon Hanson · Multiple papers, 2013–2021

Estimates of U.S. job losses from Chinese import competition post-2001 WTO accession range from 550,000 to 2.4 million. The Autor-Dorn-Hanson studies found concentrated devastation in specific communities, with lasting effects on wages, employment, and social outcomes — including increased mortality, opioid use, and political radicalization.

Klein says "around two million jobs" — this is in the middle of the published estimate range. What he doesn't mention: a 2021 reassessment found consumer price benefits offset losses for most Americans, with only 6.3% experiencing net negative effects when accounting for lower prices. The aggregate effect may have been smaller than the headline numbers suggest.

The political aftermath is well-documented: the China Shock is associated with increased support for protectionist candidates in affected regions — a phenomenon that shaped the 2016 election. Klein's claim that "we did very, very little to help them" is broadly accurate; Trade Adjustment Assistance programs were widely seen as inadequate.

Social Science Data
Time Spent With Friends — 12 Hours/Week (2003) → 5 Hours/Week (2024)
American Time Use Survey (BLS) · Jean Twenge research · Cigna Loneliness Index

The American Time Use Survey (ATUS) documents declining time spent with friends as a primary activity. Jean Twenge's research (iGen, 2017; subsequent papers) shows steep declines in in-person socializing among teens and young adults from the mid-2000s onward, correlating with smartphone adoption (2012 as a key inflection point).

The 12-hours-to-5-hours statistic likely comes from the Survey Center on American Life or a synthesis of ATUS data. The decline is real but the 12 → 5 figure's exact source should be traceable. Note that time-use surveys measure self-reported or diary-based primary activities — "time with friends" is measured differently across studies.

Debate exists about causation: does smartphone/social media use cause social isolation, or do already-isolated people use social media more? Jonathan Haidt's The Anxious Generation (2024) argues the former; critics like Candice Odgers argue the correlation is weaker than Haidt claims.

Why this deserves a hyperlink These are specific numbers Klein presents as established facts. The primary source for "12 hours in 2003" and "5 hours in 2024" should be named and linked — the measurement methodology significantly affects interpretation.
Survey Data
Teen Dating Decline — 80% (2000) → 46% (2024)
Monitoring the Future Survey (University of Michigan) · CDC Youth Risk Behavior Survey

Monitoring the Future, a longitudinal study of American youth behavior since 1975, tracks dating and romantic relationship formation. CDC's Youth Risk Behavior Survey also tracks teen relationship activity. Both show significant declines in dating and romantic activity among high school students since ~2000.

The 80% → 46% figure aligns with Twenge's data synthesis. The decline accelerates after ~2012, coinciding with smartphone ubiquity. Proposed explanations: smartphones replacing in-person interaction, dating apps making romantic norms more performance-oriented, increased anxiety and risk-aversion, helicopter parenting reducing unsupervised teen time.

This trend is alarming regardless of AI: the dating decline was well-established before large language models existed. Klein's implied connection to AI ("A.I. could be a handmaiden of this social dissolution") is speculative about future effects, not a documented current causal relationship.

Why this deserves a hyperlink This is a specific, falsifiable statistic. The surveys that document it (MTF, YRBS) are publicly accessible. The exact survey question and response categories matter for interpretation.
Survey Data
Gen Z Sexual Inactivity — ~25% Report No Sex in Past Year
General Social Survey (NORC/University of Chicago) · CDC BRFSS

The General Social Survey (GSS), conducted annually since 1972, tracks sexual frequency. The share of adults under 30 reporting no sex in the past year rose from ~8% in 2000 to ~28% by the early 2020s — with the increase concentrated among young men. CDC behavioral surveys corroborate the trend.

The GSS data is robust: it's been collected consistently for 50+ years by NORC at the University of Chicago. The trend is real and significant. Proposed explanations: delayed marriage, economic precarity (fewer men in stable jobs = fewer eligible partners), smartphone displacement of in-person meeting, and cultural shifts in how relationships form.

The gender dimension is important: the increase in sexual inactivity is disproportionately concentrated among young men, while young women's rates have been more stable. Klein doesn't mention this — it complicates the "AI as social dissolution handmaiden" framing, since the trend predates AI and has specific gendered dynamics.
